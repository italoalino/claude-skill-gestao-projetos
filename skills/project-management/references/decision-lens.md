# Decision lens (practical version)

Use this file in Mode 1 (Diagnosis). The six criteria below break ties when two answers look good. They are not the formal PMBOK 8 principles; they are practical reasoning rules, consistent with them, written for real project life.

## Contents

- The six criteria
- Ethics and transparency (always)
- Typical order of action
- Decision triggers (situation, typical response)
- Distinctions that prevent misreadings

## The six criteria

| # | Criterion | In practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Serve before directing** | Faced with a team problem, the first question is "what is blocking the team and how do I remove it", not "what order do I give". With a mature, self-organizing team, the PM protects and removes impediments; with a new team, the PM gives more structure and role clarity. |
| 2 | **Solve at the lowest possible level** | The PM acts before escalating. Escalation is a last resort, justified when the matter is beyond the PM's authority or crosses a governance threshold (for example, a financial variance above the limit, a sponsor decision). When escalating, bring facts, what has been tried and a recommendation. |
| 3 | **Be proactive** | A perceived risk, a sign of conflict or a small deviation calls for early action: understand the cause, talk, record. "Wait and see" is rarely the best option. |
| 4 | **Choose the approach by context** | Predictive, agile and hybrid are choices driven by uncertainty, delivery frequency and need for feedback. Unstable requirements and a customer who wants value early pull toward agile. Fixed, regulated scope or rigid external integration pulls toward predictive. Real projects are usually hybrid. |
| 5 | **Deliver value early and often** | Among options, prefer the one that delivers or proves value sooner, respecting governance and quality. Checking that promised benefits are being measured is almost always a good move. |
| 6 | **Tailor to context** | No process is mandatory by default. Adjust artifacts, ceremonies and cadence to size, risk and culture. "Follow all of PMBOK" is not a strategy. |

## Ethics and transparency (always)

Responsibility, respect, fairness and honesty go into every diagnosis. Warning signs:

- pressure to hide a deviation or "adjust" status numbers;
- an undeclared conflict of interest (vendor, relative, personal benefit);
- a promise to the customer the team has not validated;
- confidential information circulating in the wrong channel.

In these cases the recommendation always includes: record it, inform whoever needs to know and follow the organization's formal channel.

## Typical order of action

1. Understand: what happened, the likely cause, the impact (scope, schedule, cost, quality, risk, people).
2. Act at the PM level: direct conversation, plan adjustment, record.
3. Use the process: change request (predictive) or backlog reprioritization (agile); issue log; risk update.
4. Escalate with facts and a recommendation, if authority has run out.
5. Communicate and close the loop: confirm understanding, update documents.

## Decision triggers (situation, typical response)

| Situation | Typical response |
|---|---|
| A stakeholder acts against the project's direction | Check for a misunderstanding of the vision, clarify the cause, realign. Do not punish or escalate first. |
| Conflict with rising tension | Understand the source and context before choosing a technique. Collaborate (win-win) when there is time and the relationship matters; force only in a real emergency. |
| A conflict agreement is being broken | Reinforce the agreement directly with whoever deviated, without reopening everything and without escalating right away. |
| Mature agile team blocked by an external obstacle | Act as a servant leader and remove the impediment with the other department. |
| New team without role clarity | Set expectations and roles; more structure at first, more autonomy later. |
| Stakeholders with different needs | Tailor communication content, format and frequency per group, based on interest and influence analysis. |
| Stakeholders define success differently | Facilitate an alignment conversation before it becomes conflict. Do not change the objective to please the most influential. |
| Unrealistic stakeholder demand | Lay out the implications and propose a scope, resource or deadline adjustment. Neither accept everything nor refuse without dialogue. |
| Risk of losing knowledge | Record decisions and assumptions as they happen, not only at phase end. Avoid single points of failure. |
| Contradictory messages between areas | Reinforce the communication strategy (channels, content, frequency). More reports do not fix the wrong channel. |
| New, uncertain project | Assess need, complexity and size before recommending the approach. |
| Extra scope request on a predictive project | Record it, assess impact and run it through change control. Do not accept it outright, do not refuse without analysis. |
| Extra scope request on an agile project | It goes into the backlog, is prioritized with the product owner, and the team absorbs it in the next iteration. |
| Long project with no visible return | Consider incremental delivery to bring value forward. |
| Promised benefits without tracking | Set up or confirm a benefits measurement system. |
| Overloaded resource with a fixed date | Smooth usage within available float; level only if the date can move. |
| Uncertain scope in a procurement | Cost-reimbursable or time and materials contract; fixed price requires well-defined scope. |
| A known risk materializes | Use the contingency reserve (PM's responsibility). |
| A completely unforeseen event | Management reserve, released by governance. |
| Recurring defect | Attack the root cause in the process (quality assurance), not just more inspection. |
| Delay, budget available, low risk tolerance | Crash by adding resources to critical path activities. |
| Delay, no budget, risk accepted | Fast-track activities, knowing rework risk goes up. |
| CPI < 1 and SPI < 1 | Over budget and behind schedule: investigate the cause before acting and report with the analysis, not only the number. |
| Project ending, team will be reassigned | Capture lessons learned before releasing the team. |
| Unclear authority and escalation at the start | Define and document governance early, with escalation thresholds. |
| New regulatory requirement | Confirm it, analyze the consequences of non-compliance and adjust the plan. Compliance is continuous. |
| Approved change | Implement it, update documents and communicate status to everyone affected. |
| A risk became fact | Move it to the issue log and treat it as an active issue. |
| Many risks, little time | Prioritize with qualitative analysis (probability x impact) and quantify only what justifies it. |
| Same bottleneck across iterations | Retrospective and immediate process adjustment. |
| Resistance to organizational change | Assess culture, communicate value, involve those affected. Imposing by order rarely sustains adoption. |
| External change (market, technology, regulation) | Assess and prioritize the impact on scope and backlog, continuously. |

## Distinctions that prevent misreadings

- **Validate scope** is the customer's formal acceptance; **control quality** is internal technical verification and comes first.
- **Quality assurance** looks at the process; **quality control** looks at the deliverable.
- **Scope creep** is uncontrolled addition requested from outside; **gold plating** is the team delivering more than asked. Both are undesirable.
- **Crashing** adds resources (cost goes up); **fast tracking** runs work in parallel (risk goes up).
- **Contingency reserve** covers known risks and sits in the cost baseline; **management reserve** covers unknowns and sits outside it.
- **Risk** is uncertain and future; an **issue** has already happened and affects today.
- **Aligning** expectations is the initial convergence; **managing** expectations is sustaining and responding over time.
- **Organizational change management** is about people's adoption; **change control** is about changes to the project plan.
