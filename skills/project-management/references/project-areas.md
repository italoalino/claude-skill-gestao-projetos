# Project areas: what to look at in each one

A practical guide by area, written from project management practice and aligned with the areas the PMBOK® Guide covers. It does not reproduce or replace the guide: for the official description of processes, inputs and outputs, consult the PMBOK® Guide.

Nothing here is a mandatory sequence. Tailor it to the size, risk and culture of the organization.

## Governance and change

**The area's question:** who decides what, within which limits, and how does the project prove it delivers value?

**Typical artifacts:** charter, integrated plan, change log, performance report, lessons learned, closure report.

**Practical cautions:**
- The charter authorizes the project and names the PM. The sponsor approves it. It is not the place to detail scope and schedule.
- Plans that conflict with each other are fixed by integrating them, not by adjusting one dimension in isolation (changing only the deadline, for example).
- A change is implemented only after approval. Before that, assess the impact on scope, schedule, cost, quality, risk and benefit.
- Quality assurance looks at the process; quality control looks at the deliverable.
- Record lessons during the project, not only at the end. Tacit knowledge moves through conversation, not only documents.
- Close with formal acceptance and capture the lessons before releasing the team.
- Data becomes information when someone decides with it. Measurement: EVM in predictive work; burn-up, velocity and backlog in agile.
- A flexible contract suits an adaptive project; fixed price requires well-defined scope.

## Scope

**The area's question:** what is in and out of the project, and who accepts the deliverable?

**Typical artifacts:** requirements, scope statement, WBS, backlog, acceptance criteria.

**Practical cautions:**
- A requirement is not a solution. Separate business need, stakeholder need, solution requirement, transition requirement and quality requirement.
- The WBS breaks down deliverables, not activities. In agile the equivalent is epic, feature and story, with slicing.
- Scope creep is uncontrolled addition requested from outside; gold plating is the team delivering more than asked. Both are problems.
- Validating scope is the customer's acceptance and comes after internal quality control.
- In adaptive work, product vision, an ordered backlog and a definition of done play the role of the scope statement.

## Schedule

**The area's question:** when does it deliver, and what supports that date?

**Typical artifacts:** schedule with critical path, milestones, roadmap, dependency map.

**Practical cautions:**
- Only shortening the critical path shortens the project.
- Compressing with resources (crashing) raises cost; running work in parallel (fast tracking) raises the risk of rework.
- External dependencies need an owner and a confirmation date.
- In agile, the forecast is high level (roadmap and release) and is adjusted every cycle from actual velocity.

## Finance

**The area's question:** how much does it cost, how much has been spent and what is the forecast at completion?

**Typical artifacts:** estimates and their basis, budget, cost baseline, forecasts at completion.

**Practical cautions:**
- Analogous estimating is cheap and less accurate; bottom-up is more accurate and more expensive. Accuracy grows as the project progresses.
- The cost baseline is the sum of the estimates plus the contingency reserve. The management reserve (for the unforeseen) sits outside it.
- Define who controls each reserve and which thresholds trigger escalation.
- CPI below 1 means over budget. Investigate the cause before reporting only the number.
- In agile, track spend per iteration and review funding every cycle.

## Stakeholders and communication

**The area's question:** who is affected, what do they expect and how engaged are they?

**Typical artifacts:** stakeholder register, engagement plan, communication plan.

**Practical cautions:**
- Identifying stakeholders is continuous, not a one-time step. Compare current engagement with desired engagement.
- The register circulates. Do not put sensitive data or personal opinions about people in it.
- The number of communication channels grows fast with group size (n x (n - 1) / 2). Prefer a few clear channels.
- Choose the channel by urgency and by the need for dialogue. More reports do not fix the wrong channel.
- Managing is acting; monitoring is checking whether the strategy works. In conflict, facing the problem is usually better than working around it.

## Resources and team

**The area's question:** who does the work, with what availability, and what is blocking them?

**Typical artifacts:** resource plan, responsibility matrix (RACI), availability calendar.

**Practical cautions:**
- A single accountable person per deliverable.
- Resources include materials, environments and equipment, not only people.
- A new team needs more structure and role clarity; a mature team needs impediments removed. Leading means adapting the style to the stage.
- Overload and dependence on a single person are risks, not calendar details.
- In agile, a dedicated, stable team delivers more than people split across many projects.

## Risk

**The area's question:** what could change the outcome, and what are we doing about it?

**Typical artifacts:** risk management plan, risk register, planned responses.

**Practical cautions:**
- Record threats and opportunities. Write them as "cause, event, effect".
- Qualitative analysis always; quantitative only when the effort pays off.
- A planned response that is not carried out does not reduce exposure. Give it an owner and a date.
- A risk that happens leaves the risk register and becomes an issue.
- Define the sponsor's risk appetite before prioritizing.

## How the areas chain together

A risk that materializes becomes an issue, which may require a change request, which affects scope, schedule and cost. Compliance and sustainability cut across every area and deserve a question at the start of any project.
